# apps/tower_defence

`src/apps/tower_defence/` — a campaign of levels generated by constraint solving, and waves of mobs walked along them.

## What it demonstrates

[`wfc`](../libraries/wfc.md) generating levels whose path is guaranteed linear, plus the ordinary tick/replay stack drawing and taking clicks — and a persisted high score read through the same versioned-document pipeline every other saved file here uses.

The interesting part is that WFC is a constraint solver, not a path guarantee: "one non-intersecting path from start to end" is a *global* property, and plain adjacency rules will happily break it.

## Running it

```sh
build/bin/antwika_tower_defence/antwika_tower_defence
build/bin/antwika_tower_defence/antwika_tower_defence --record demo.replay
build/bin/antwika_tower_defence/antwika_tower_defence --replay src/apps/tower_defence/replays/demo.jsonl
```

It opens a window, draws the level each tick and takes mouse input.
Like [life](life.md) it has no end of its own: it runs until the window is closed or a replay dispatches `engine.stop`, and a headless build reports neither — so `Ctrl+C` ends one there, and a `--record` run killed that way keeps every event up to the kill.
Clearing the last wave of the last level, or running out of lives, ends the *campaign* rather than the run: the score stops moving, the bar says which of the two happened, and the window stays open.

## The campaign

`campaignLevels()` in `Campaign.cpp` is the whole of it: three levels, played in order, sharing one score and one pool of lives.

| Level | Grid | Wall spacing | Tower reach | Tower damage | What arrives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The approach | 12x8 | every 3rd column | 4 (two cells) | 1 | grunts, then runners |
| The narrows | 10x10 | every 2nd column | 2 (one cell) | 2 | brutes, runners, the first shielded |
| The gauntlet | 14x9 | every 4th column | 8 (two cells) | 2 | everything, and a lot of it |

**A level differs in how it plays, not only in where the road runs**, and that is the point of the table having five columns rather than one.
Wall spacing decides how much the path weaves and therefore how much open ground is left to build on.
Tower reach decides whether a corner covers a stretch of road or only the cell beside it — the narrows cut it to one cell, so a tower there has to hug the road.
Tower damage is what makes armour mean anything at all, which is why the first shielded mob does not appear until the level whose guns hit for two.
`CampaignTest.TheShippedCampaignHasLevelsThatPlayDifferently` asserts that no two levels share both their grid and their guns, so a "new level" that is a re-roll of an old one fails the build.

**The reason to progress is that lives and score both carry and towers do not.**
A campaign starts with `kStartingLives` (12); every mob that reaches the end costs one, and running out ends the campaign where it stands.
A cleared level is followed immediately by the next, on a fresh grid — so every tower built on the level before is gone, and the money-free economy here is really an economy of clicks and of the lives left to spend on learning a layout.

## The mob kinds

`MobKind.hpp` names four, and `MobKind.cpp` holds the one table they are balanced by.

| Kind | Ticks per cell | Health | Armour | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grunt | 2 | 6 | 0 | 10 |
| Runner | 1 | 4 | 0 | 14 |
| Brute | 3 | 18 | 0 | 24 |
| Shielded | 2 | 8 | 1 | 30 |

The four axes pull against each other rather than ranking:

- **Ticks per cell is the axis that interacts with where a tower goes.** The damage a mob takes crossing a reach is the cells in reach times the ticks it spends in each, so a Brute at three pays a corner tower three times what a Runner at one does. A tower that is fine against brutes can be walked straight past by runners.
- **Health and reward are the ordinary dials**, and they are what make a Brute worth waiting for and a Runner worth catching early.
- **Armour is flat damage reduction, taken off every hit, and the result never heals.** A tower whose damage does not exceed a mob's armour cannot hurt it *at all*, and no number of towers changes that, since each tower fires on its own. That is a real trap rather than a hidden one: `campaignLevels()` only fields an armoured kind on a level whose guns out-damage it, and `CampaignTest.NoLevelFieldsAKindItsOwnGunsCannotHurt` asserts that of the shipped table.

Each kind is drawn in its own colour (`BattleScene.cpp`), because which of the four is on the road is the one thing a player has to read off the grid rather than off the bar.

## The waves

A level is a `std::vector<Wave>`, and a `Wave` is a *composition* rather than a sequence: so many of this kind and so many of that, a `spawnPeriodTicks` of quiet between one mob and the next, and a `gapTicks` of quiet after the last of them.

`planWaves()` turns each composition into a `WaveRelease` — a flat spawn order — with a Fisher-Yates shuffle, **once, before the level starts**, from a generator seeded off the campaign's seed.
Two things follow from doing it there rather than per tick:

1. Nothing inside the tick path ever draws a random number, so a battle is a pure function of its level, its releases and the ticks stepped.
2. The order is still reproducible from the seed, which is what a replay needs — it carries no wave and no mob, only the clicks.

The shuffle's arithmetic is written out rather than handed to `std::shuffle` for [`rng`](../libraries/rng.md)'s reason: the standard distributions are not portable between standard libraries, so a shuffle through one would deal a different wave under libstdc++ and libc++ and break any recording that crossed toolchains.

Finishing the last wave is what completes a level: `Battle::cleared()` is "every wave released and nothing left alive", and `Campaign::step()` builds the next level on the tick that becomes true.

## The high score

`tower_defence_highscore.json`, in the working directory rather than beside the executable — a high score is what somebody's run made, not a shipped asset, so it does not belong in the directory a build writes.
The path is `main()`'s decision and is never defaulted inside `FileScoreStore`, which would pick the same file for every run.

```json
{
  "magic": "antwika-tower-defence-score",
  "version": 1,
  "bestScore": 340,
  "bestLevel": 3
}
```

Two numbers rather than one: the score alone cannot tell a run that scraped through three levels from one that farmed the first, and how far somebody got is the other half of "best".
`bestOf()` decides on the score, and the level comes along with whichever score won — saying a better-scoring run got less far would be reporting two runs as one.

**It is read as `parse -> read version -> migrate -> validate -> decode`**, through `replay::readVersionedDocument` and an injected `replay::MigrationChain`, exactly as [`docs/schema-versioning.md`](../../docs/schema-versioning.md) requires.
`standardScoreMigrations()` hands back a chain with **no steps in it**, because this format has one revision and writing a migration for a version that never shipped would be fiction.
The chain is still what reads every document, so the first bump is one entry in that list and nothing else — and until then it is what refuses a file from a build newer than this one.
The `"magic"` is what tells this format from the replay document, the game save and the companion's, since all four state their version in the same `"version"` member and nothing else distinguishes them.
It is the *schema* that checks it, though, so it is checked **after** the migration chain rather than before it — and a file of one of those other formats stating a version this build cannot reach is therefore refused as coming from a newer build rather than as not being a score file at all.
Naming it correctly would mean reading the magic ahead of the version, which none of the four formats does today.

**A missing high score is an ordinary answer, not an error.**
`FileScoreStore::load()` returns `std::nullopt` for a file that is not there, which is a first run and worth a best of zero; an absent file never stops a game.
A file that *is* there and will not read is a `ScoreFormatError`, and `bootstrap()` logs it, starts the run from nothing and goes on to write over it — keeping the unreadable one would be kinder to whoever wants to look at it and would leave the application never able to save again, which is worse.
A record that will not *write* is thrown, since the run is already over and the one thing left to say is that it was not kept.

## How linearity is guaranteed

It is arranged in three layers rather than checked for afterwards.

1. **The alphabet cannot express a junction.** No symbol in `LevelTile.hpp` is open on more than two sides, so a T-junction or a crossroads does not exist as a value.
2. **Exactly one `Start` and one `End`, both on a border.** The solution is then a union of simple cycles plus exactly one simple path, whose two ends are the only degree-one cells — so walking out of `Start` always arrives at `End`.
3. **Stray cycles are erased rather than rejected.** Any cell the walk misses is a cycle nothing uses, and `generateLevel()` deletes it, so generation never reseeds for the sake of linearity.

`Tile::Empty` is symbol 0 because `wfc::Solver` tries candidates in ascending order, and wall columns with one gap each keep the grid connected while forcing the path to weave.

A tight per-attempt step budget with many reseeds beat one large budget by roughly twenty times: a hard seed is cheaper to abandon than to grind out.

**The property is a property of the alphabet, not of one grid size**, which is what lets a campaign field three differently-shaped levels.
`LevelGeneratorTest.EveryShippedLevelIsGenerableAndLinear` sweeps every level in `campaignLevels()`, so a fourth level added to that table is soaked for free.

## How it is put together

`td::Campaign` is the simulation — integer throughout, no clock and no global generator, so it is a pure function of the tick count, the state and one seed.
It owns the `Battle` being fought rather than being one, because a battle is replaced wholesale when a level is cleared: anything holding one by reference would be left pointing at the level before, which is why every sink here holds the `Campaign`.
`td::Battle` is one level's fight, and keeps no score and no lives of its own — those span the levels, so `Battle::step()` reports what its tick earned and let through and `Campaign` folds it in.

Rendering is a write-only projection in structure rather than by promise: `td::snapshotOf()` takes an immutable `BattleSnapshot`, `BattleScene` turns that into drawing calls, and `RenderSink` runs it once per `engine.tick`, registered after `CampaignSink` and `ScoreSink` so a frame is of the state the tick ended with.

The bar is drawn by [`ui`](../libraries/ui.md), described by `td::ScoreSink` inside the tick path and painted from `td::ScoreOverlay`, so no `ui.*` event exists here either.
Every word on it goes through [`i18n`](../libraries/i18n.md) as a `MessageId`, and `main()` fixes the locale at `kDefaultLocale`: the bar is laid out from translated text, and a locale read from an environment variable or a flag is not something a recording carries.

`td::GridLayout.hpp` is the one place the pixel-to-cell mapping lives, shared by the scene and the placement sink so what somebody sees and what they can build on cannot drift.

## Non-obvious decisions

**The app defines no event for placing a tower.**
A click is the input; `TowerPlacementSink` turns it into a placement inside the tick path, and the replay stores the click and regenerates the tower.
Persisting both would build two towers per click.

**The pixel-to-cell mapping is worked out per press rather than once.**
It used to be computed in the sink's constructor, which was right while every run had one grid.
A campaign's levels are not all the same size, so the mapping is now derived from whichever level is being fought — and both that and the canvas are state a replay regenerates, so the mapping regenerates with them.

**A tower's target needs an explicit tie-break, and now has one.**
Mobs used to advance one cell per tick each, so no two could ever share a path index and "the first in reach" was always the furthest along.
Kinds walk at different paces now: two mobs can stand on one cell, and a Runner can overtake a Brute.
So `Battle::fire()` picks the greatest path index and breaks a tie by the smallest mob id, both total orders, and `BattleTest.TheFurthestAlongIsShotAndATieGoesToTheOlderMob` pins both halves.

**The previous best is run configuration, not simulation state.**
It is read once, before the loop starts, and handed in the way the canvas and the seed are — never from inside the tick path, where a file's answer could differ between a live run and a replay of it.
Nothing on the bar is clickable and nothing is hit-tested against it, so a run started against a different best is still the same simulation.
A replay is handed no store at all (`storeIfLive()`), for the reason `companion::storeIfLive()` gives: a record loaded from whatever happens to be on the machine running a recording is a number that recording never saw, and writing one back would let a replayed run overwrite a record it did not earn.

**The score bar stays one row however much it says.**
The strip reserved above the grid is `td::scoreBarHeight(canvas)`, the height one row of labels actually lays out to from the same theme padding and glyph metrics.
A second row would leave the bar covering grid rows a click could still reach, so the campaign's outcome replaces the wave counter in the middle of the row rather than being added below it.

Reserving that strip is also why a click on the bar builds nothing: it falls outside the grid, so no sink has to ask the UI whether it covered the pointer.

**The wide seed sweep runs in an optimised build only.**
`LevelGeneratorTest` asserts the linear-path property over forty seeds, but generating one level costs about 2.9 s under the coverage build's `-O0` against 0.14 s at `-O2`, and forty of them was a third of the entire CI test step.
`src/apps/tower_defence/tests/CMakeLists.txt` sets three seed counts from `ENABLE_COVERAGE`: eight for the linearity soak under instrumentation and forty otherwise, two for the cases reading some other property, and one per shipped level for the campaign sweep, since that one is a whole generation per level.
CI runs the wide sweep in the GNU leg's "Soak the level generator" step, which builds that one target against `conan-release`.
So the coverage legs prove the coverage and an optimised build proves the property, and neither pays for the other.

**Everything else a campaign test needs is fought on a five-by-three grid.**
Generating is the only expensive thing this application does, and what those cases are about is what happens *between* levels, so they ask for the smallest grid the generator will answer for rather than the shipped one.
